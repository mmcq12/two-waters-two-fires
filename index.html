Two waters, two fires

Proto-Indo-European seems to have kept one word for water the substance, and another for water the living force. Fire too.

Water, the thing*wódr̥ Water, the being*h₂ékʷeh₂ Fire, the thing*péh₂wr̥ Fire, the being*h₁n̥gʷnis

Solid boxes are inanimate nouns. Dashed boxes are animate ones.

Antoine Meillet spotted the pattern a century ago. The “thing” words are neuter: grammatically inert, like a stone. The “being” words are animate: they can act, be addressed, be worshipped.

Hittite, the oldest attested branch, still sorts its nouns this way: common versus neuter, no masculine or feminine at all.

It is an elegant hypothesis, widely repeated. It is not a theorem. But the descendants make a persuasive witness list.

Water, the thing *wódr̥

Water as stuff: something you pour, measure, get wet in.

water, wet, wash English

All three from the same root. English is, on this evidence, a thoroughly inanimate-water language.

wātar Hittite

Second millennium BCE, on clay. Near enough to English “water” to be faintly unnerving.

hýdōr Greek

Hence hydrogen, hydraulic, the Hydra, and the clepsydra: a water clock, literally a “water thief”.

unda Latin, “wave”

Hence undulate, inundate, abundant and redundant: “overflowing”, and “overflowing again”. Redundant is, fittingly, more than it needs to be.

Also surround, from Late Latin superundare, “to overflow”. It has nothing whatever to do with round.

undine Paracelsus

Coined from unda for his water elementals. Ironic: a spirit named with the inanimate word.

otter English

The “water creature”. Cousin of the Greek hydra, which is to say the otter has an unexpectedly mythological family.

vodka, whisky Russian, Irish

Vodka is “little water”. Whisky is uisce beatha, “water of life”.

Order a whisky and water and you have ordered water, twice. The barman need not be told.

Water, the being *h₂ékʷeh₂ / *h₂ep-

Water as agent: the river that moves, gives, takes.

Possibly two roots rather than one. Both sit firmly on the animate side.

aqua Latin

Hence acqua, agua, eau, aquarium, aqueduct. Romance chose the living water.

ēa Old English, “river”

Fossilised in island: īegland, “water-land”. The silent s is a Renaissance blunder, borrowed from the unrelated Latin insula.

á Old Norse, “river”

Still flowing through Scandinavian river names as the single letter å. Economy of means.

āpas Vedic Sanskrit

Usually plural, and feminine: “the Waters”, addressed in the Rigveda as goddesses. Very much not a stone.

Punjab Persian

Panj āb, “five waters”: the five rivers. Persian āb descends from *h₂ep-.

Fire, the thing *péh₂wr̥

Fire as stuff: the contents of the grate.

fire, Feuer English, German

Germanic took the inert fire, as it took the inert water. A practical people.

pŷr Greek

Hence pyre, pyrite, pyrotechnics, and the empyrean: the highest heaven, “in the fire”.

paḫḫur Hittite

Neuter, as predicted. Hittite remains an obliging witness.

fōn Gothic

The same r/n split as water. English fire kept the r. Gothic kept the n: fōn, genitive funins.

Fire, the being *h₁n̥gʷnis

Fire as agent: the thing that might decide to eat your house.

agní Vedic Sanskrit

Agni, the fire god, messenger between humans and gods.

The Rigveda’s very first word is agním: “I praise Agni.” The animate fire opens the oldest Indic text.

ignis Latin

Hence ignite, igneous, ignition. Note what ignis did not become: the ordinary Romance word for fire.

ugnis, ogon’ Lithuanian, Russian

Balto-Slavic kept the living fire as its everyday word. Polish too: ogień.

Which ancestor?

Tap the root each word descends from.

The god retires

Italian fuoco, French feu, Spanish fuego: none descends from ignis.

They come from Latin focus, “hearth”. Romance put the god out and kept the fireplace.

English did something similar, more thoroughly. Both its everyday words are the inert ones. The spirits survive only as loans, and one stowaway in island.
